# Ask for the Cook — Myanmar Restaurant Group Retention Campaign ★ full client-ready

**Sector.** F&B — restaurant group, multi-outlet, dine-in + delivery

**Market.** Myanmar (Yangon, Mandalay)

**Type.** Integrated retention campaign — engineered for the second visit

## 1. CAMPAIGN OVERVIEW

Myanmar restaurant marketing is a discount treadmill. Platform promo, 20% off,
footfall spikes, margin drops, the customer eats once and never returns because
nothing except the price was memorable. Next month the promo runs again, deeper.

The economics are brutal and well understood by the operator: a first visit
bought at 20% off is barely break-even. **Every peso of profit in this business
lives in the second visit, and almost nobody markets for it.**

**Objective.** Lift 60-day repeat rate from baseline by **14 points** across the
group, raise delivery re-order rate by 18%, and reduce average discount depth by
5 points — over 16 weeks, without buying footfall.

**Period.** 16 weeks. **Budget envelope.** MMK 58,000,000.

**The bet.** A discount is forgettable by design. A person is not.

## 2. TARGET AUDIENCE PROFILE

### Segment A — The Repeat-Capable Diner (the whole campaign)
**Demographics.** 24–42, office workers and small-business owners, MMK 500k–2M
monthly household income, eats out or orders 4–10 times a month.
**Location.** Within 4km of an outlet — Yangon (Bahan, Kamayut, Sanchaung,
Hlaing), Mandalay Chan Aye Thar Zan.
**Psychographics.** Has 6–8 places in rotation and rarely adds one. Adds a place
when something specific happened, not because it was cheap.
**Behaviours.** Decides in 40 seconds on a delivery app. Screenshots and shares
food. Returns to places where staff recognise them.
**Pain points.** Inconsistency — the dish is different on the second visit;
nobody to tell when something is wrong; the discount ends and so does the reason.
**Media.** Facebook 12–1 PM and 7–10 PM, TikTok evenings, delivery app browsing
at decision moment, Viber with friends.

### Segment B — The Group Organiser (highest ticket)
**Demographics.** 28–45, chooses the venue for 6–15 people — office lunch,
family Sunday, birthday.
**Psychographics.** Carries social risk. If the venue disappoints, it is *their*
recommendation that failed.
**Pain points.** No confidence the kitchen holds up under a big order; booking is
a phone call that may not be answered; no way to pre-agree the menu.
**Media.** Facebook, Viber groups, direct calls.

### Segment C — The Kitchen Staff (internal, and non-negotiable)
**Demographics.** 19–45, line cooks, sous chefs, station leads. Often invisible
in the brand.
**Psychographics.** Trained, proud, and never named. High turnover partly because
the work is anonymous.
**Why they are a campaign audience.** This campaign puts their names in public.
If they are not brought in first, as participants and not as content, it fails —
and it deserves to.

## 3. COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE

| Who | What they do | The gap we take |
|---|---|---|
| Delivery-platform-led restaurants | Promo depth, platform ranking | Buys visits, builds no relationship, margin bleeds |
| Big local chains | Consistency, locations, brand recall | Anonymous, industrial, no reason to feel loyal |
| Independent "chef" restaurants | A named owner-chef, personality | One person, one outlet, cannot scale the intimacy |
| International franchises | Systems and reliability | Culturally distant, priced above the segment |

Nobody has scaled *personal* accountability across multiple outlets. The
independents have it and cannot grow it; the chains grew and lost it. That gap is
the whole opportunity.

## 4. CAMPAIGN STRATEGY

### The big idea — "Ask for the Cook" / "စားဖိုမှူးကို မေးပါ"

Every dish that leaves the kitchen carries the name of the person who cooked it —
on the table card, on the receipt, on the delivery bag sticker. And the invitation
is explicit: **if it was good, ask for them by name. If it was not, ask for them
anyway.**

The cook is no longer an anonymous function behind a wall. They are a person you
can request, thank, or complain to. That is a reason to come back that a 20%
discount structurally cannot produce — and a competitor cannot copy it without
first being willing to let their kitchen be named.

**Why this and not the obvious one.** The obvious campaign is slow-motion food, a
sizzle, and "Taste the Difference". It is beautiful, it is interchangeable, and it
drives a first visit at best. Naming the cook changes the *product*, not the
advertising — which is why it survives the campaign ending.

**Brand positioning.** The kitchen you can talk to.

**Key messages.**
- "Cooked by [name]." — on every dish, every channel, every outlet.
- "Ask for them next time." — the return mechanic, stated as an invitation.
- "Tell them yourself." — complaints routed to the kitchen, answered publicly.
- "Same hands, same dish." — consistency framed as a person, not a process.

**Tone of voice.** Warm, specific, unpolished. Burmese written first, everyday
register. Real kitchen sound. No food-porn narration, no "culinary journey".

**Hashtags.** Primary `#စားဖိုမှူးကိုမေးပါ` · Secondary `#AskForTheCook` `#CookedBy`

### The internal move that makes it real

Before a single ad runs: cooks opt in by name, are paid a **recognition
allowance** when requested by customers, and are trained on the complaint route.
If the kitchen does not want this, the campaign does not run. We would rather
say that in week one than discover it in week six.

## 9. ACTIVATION — THE COOK'S TABLE

Once a month, at rotating outlets, **The Cook's Table**: twelve seats, one cook,
one menu they chose themselves, and they serve it. Guests book by the cook's
name. No host, no MC, no speeches — the cook talks about the food or does not.

Four across the campaign. Priced at cost, capped at twelve, always full within a
day. It is the campaign's most photographed asset and its cheapest.

## 13. ROI PROJECTION

Group baseline: 42,000 covers/month, average ticket MMK 14,500, contribution
margin 58%. A 14-point repeat lift is modelled on the repeat-capable base only.

| Scenario | Repeat lift | Incremental covers/mo | Year-1 contribution (MMK) | Return on 123M |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | +8 pts | 2,240 | 226,000,000 | 1.8× |
| Base | +14 pts | 3,920 | 395,000,000 | 3.2× |
| Upside | +19 pts | 5,320 | 536,000,000 | 4.4× |

Plus the discount effect: a 5-point reduction in average discount depth on
existing volume is worth roughly **MMK 106,000,000 a year** in recovered margin on
its own — and unlike the repeat lift, it starts the week the promos are cut.

## 14. RISK & MITIGATION

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Kitchen staff do not want to be named | Medium | **High** | Opt-in only, week 1, before any spend. If uptake is below 60% the campaign does not launch — we will not pressure staff into being content. | ZYNTH + client HR |
| A named cook receives abuse from a customer | Medium | **High** | Complaints route to the manager first, never directly to the cook; publicly answered by the brand, not the individual; named cooks are never exposed to a public pile-on | Client ops |
| Consistency review finds real inconsistency | **High** | Medium | Publish it and publish the fix. If consistency is badly broken, delay the review until month 3 — but do not cancel it. | ZYNTH |
| A named cook leaves mid-campaign | **High** | Low | Content is portfolio, not dependency; departures acknowledged warmly, never hidden | Client ops |
| Discount withdrawal drops volume before repeat lift arrives | Medium | High | Taper discounts over weeks 3–8 rather than cutting; watch weekly covers, restore if drop exceeds 12% | Client + ZYNTH |
| Delivery platform ranking falls without promo | **High** | Medium | Offset with in-app content placement and re-order prompts, budgeted in §8 | ZYNTH |

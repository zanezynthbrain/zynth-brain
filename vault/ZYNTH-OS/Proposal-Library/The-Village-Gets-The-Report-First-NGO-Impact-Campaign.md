# The Village Gets the Report First — NGO & Social Impact Communications Campaign ★ full client-ready

**Sector.** NGO & Social Impact

**Market.** Myanmar (programme areas) + Singapore / regional institutional donors

**Type.** Integrated communications campaign — community accountability, donor confidence, volunteer and staff recruitment

## 1. CAMPAIGN OVERVIEW

Every NGO in Myanmar produces an annual report. It is written in English, laid
out for a donor, published on a website the beneficiaries do not read, and
distributed to people who were not there.

The community that lived the programme is the last to hear how it went — if they
hear at all. And the donor, reading a document produced by the organisation being
evaluated, quietly discounts it.

Both audiences are being under-served by the same habit.

**Objective.** Raise institutional donor confidence enough to convert **4 renewals
and 3 new multi-year commitments**, lift qualified volunteer applications by 140%,
and establish community reporting as the organisation's signature practice — over
20 weeks.

**Period.** 20 weeks. **Budget envelope.** MMK 46,000,000 (~S$13,900).

**The bet.** An organisation that reports to the community *before* the donor is
making a claim about itself no press release can make — and it is the single
strongest thing a donor can be shown.

## 2. TARGET AUDIENCE PROFILE

### Segment A — The Institutional Donor / Programme Officer
**Demographics.** 30–50, programme or grants officer at an international
foundation, bilateral agency, regional CSR fund, or Singapore family office.
Decides or recommends on grants of USD 50k–2M.
**Location.** Singapore, Bangkok, Yangon country offices, remote.
**Psychographics.** Personally answerable for outcomes they cannot observe
directly. Reads dozens of near-identical reports. Deeply sceptical of
self-assessment and of numbers with no method attached.
**Pain points.** Cannot verify claimed impact; partners over-promise then
under-report; no view of what failed; due-diligence burden with weak evidence;
reputational exposure if a partner is later found to have overstated.
**Media.** Email, LinkedIn, sector convenings, peer referral. Reads long
documents when they contain something new.

### Segment B — The Community / Programme Participant
**Demographics.** Adults 20–60 in programme townships; mixed literacy; often the
household decision-maker on participation.
**Psychographics.** Has seen organisations arrive, photograph, and leave. Judges
credibility by whether someone came back and explained.
**Pain points.** No idea what happened to the programme after it ended; no channel
to say it did not work; asked for data repeatedly and never shown the result.
**Media.** Facebook (mobile), Viber groups, community radio in some areas,
in-person meetings above all.

### Segment C — The Volunteer / Prospective Staff
**Demographics.** 21–34, graduates and early-career, Yangon and Mandalay.
**Psychographics.** Wants meaningful work but has heard the sector described as
politically fraught and administratively hollow. Looking for evidence of rigour.
**Pain points.** Cannot tell serious organisations from performative ones; no
visibility of what the work actually involves day to day.
**Media.** Facebook, LinkedIn, TikTok, university networks.

### Segment D — The Domestic Individual Donor
**Demographics.** 28–55, Myanmar professionals and business owners who give.
**Pain points.** "Where did the money go" is unanswered; prefers to give directly
to people they know rather than to an institution they cannot audit.
**Media.** Facebook, Viber, word of mouth.

## 3. COMPETITIVE LANDSCAPE

"Competition" here is for donor confidence and public trust, not market share.

| Who | How they communicate | The gap we take |
|---|---|---|
| Large INGOs | Polished English annual reports, professional photography | Community never sees it; reads as institutional, not accountable |
| Local CSOs | Facebook activity updates, event photos | No method, no outcome measurement, no failure reporting |
| Direct-giving campaigns | Individual stories, emotional appeal, fast | Cannot demonstrate systemic outcome or sustained delivery |
| Corporate CSR arms | Brand-led, campaign-shaped | Short-horizon, rarely independently verified |

Nobody in this landscape reports to the community first. It costs little and it
is the one practice a competitor cannot adopt without genuinely changing how they
operate.

## 4. CAMPAIGN STRATEGY

### The big idea — "The Village Gets the Report First" / "အစီရင်ခံစာကို ရွာက အရင်ရမယ်"

Invert the reporting order. Every programme cycle closes with a **community
report-back**: the organisation returns to the township, in Burmese, in person,
and presents what was spent, what was achieved, what was missed, and what will
change. The community responds — on record. Only *then* does the donor report go
out, and it carries the community's response inside it.

The donor document stops being a self-assessment. It becomes a transcript.

**Why this and not the obvious one.** The obvious campaign is beneficiary
portraits, a hopeful score, and "Together We Can". Every organisation in the
sector runs it, which is precisely why a donor discounts it. Reporting to the
community first is a *behaviour*, not a message — it produces evidence as a
by-product, and an organisation unwilling to hear the response in public simply
cannot copy it.

**Brand positioning.** The organisation that answers to the people it serves,
before the people who fund it.

**Key messages.**
- "The village hears it first." — the reporting order, stated plainly.
- "Including what did not work." — a named failure in every cycle report.
- "Here is the method." — measurement approach published alongside every number.
- "Ask us on the record." — an open community question channel with published answers.

**Tone of voice.** Plain, accountable, unsentimental. **Burmese written first**,
English transcreated — for this campaign that is not only house rule but the
argument itself. No rescue framing, no beneficiaries described as passive, no
imagery that trades on distress.

**Hashtags.** Primary `#ရွာကအရင်သိရမယ်` · Secondary `#VillageFirstReport` `#AskUsOnTheRecord`

## 9. ACTIVATION — THE REPORT-BACKS

**Four community report-backs** across 20 weeks, one per programme township.
Format, fixed and published in advance:

1. What we said we would do (10 min, Burmese, printed one-page handout)
2. What we spent (10 min, itemised, no aggregate hiding)
3. What we achieved and what we did not (20 min)
4. **Open floor, recorded and published** (40 min)
5. What changes next cycle, committed on the spot (10 min)

Plus one **Donor Field Day** — Segment A donors attend a report-back as
observers, not guests. They watch the open floor. Nothing is rehearsed for them.

## 13. ROI PROJECTION

This is not a revenue campaign; return is measured in funding secured and
retained. Average institutional grant: USD 180,000 over two years.

| Scenario | Renewals | New commitments | Funding secured (USD) | Return on ~S$34k |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Conservative | 3 | 1 | 720,000 | 15× |
| Base | 4 | 3 | 1,260,000 | 27× |
| Upside | 5 | 5 | 1,800,000 | 38× |

**Stated carefully:** these are funding outcomes influenced by communications, not
caused by them alone. Programme quality, existing relationships and donor cycles
drive most of the result. The honest claim is that this campaign removes the
verification doubt that currently slows or shrinks decisions — it does not
manufacture funding, and any agency telling you otherwise is selling you a
number.

## 14. RISK & MITIGATION

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation | Owner |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Do no harm** — published material identifies individuals or locations in ways that create risk for participants | Medium | **Severe** | Protection review before every publication; no GPS, no identifying detail, no faces without informed consent recorded in Burmese; participants may withdraw at any time, including after publication. Any doubt means the asset is not published. | ZYNTH + client protection lead |
| Community response at open floor is severely critical | **High** | Medium | This is the design working, not failing. Publish it and publish the commitment made in response. Rehearsing the floor would forfeit the entire asset. | Client leadership |
| Organisation is not operationally ready to answer publicly | Medium | High | Week-1 readiness assessment. If the programme cannot survive an open floor, fix delivery before communications. We will not stage a report-back. | ZYNTH + client |
| Donor reads published failures as poor performance | Medium | Medium | Frame in the method annex from the first contact; brief donors before the first publication, never after | Client partnerships |

## 16. WHY ZYNTH

This campaign asks an organisation to publish its failures and record the
criticism of the people it serves. Most agencies will steer a client away from
that within ten minutes, because comfortable work is easier to sell and easier to
approve. We are proposing it because in this sector the discomfort *is* the
credential — and because a donor who watches an unrehearsed open floor stops
needing to be convinced.

What we have actually produced: a 20-section fintech launch proposal with full
segmentation and scenario modelling; a premium launch event proposal with a
complete vendor and risk register; manufacturing, logistics and energy campaigns
built on the same published-evidence mechanic; a spec product film to commercial
sound standards; eight 3D stage and exhibition concepts.

What we do not claim: an NGO client, or field protection expertise. This would be
our first in the sector, and the protection review in §14 must be led by the
client's own safeguarding lead, not by us. What we bring is the method, the
bilingual production capacity, and a refusal to make the work look better than it
is.
